Field guide

Navigating the atlas

Move through the solar system at any pace, from any point of view. Every orbit is computed, not drawn by hand.

Click

Click a world to center the map; on touch, tap a world or its label. The Sun, every planet, Pluto and the Moon are valid centers. Tapping an orbit line away from a marker opens its date card.

Drag

Pan the map. In Orbits, Shift + drag on desktop or drag with two fingers on touch to tilt and rotate. Pinch or scroll to zoom; arrow keys pan, and Shift + arrows tilt on desktop.

Scroll

Zoom around the pointer. Pinch with two fingers on a touchscreen.

Hover

Hover any orbit line on desktop, or tap an orbit line on touch, to see the UTC date and time when that body occupied that spot. Tap elsewhere to dismiss it.

Modes

Orbits is a tilted, explorable true-scale view. Overview is top-down at true scale with no radial compression; it uses the same Solid, Fading and Always line styles and History controls as Orbits. Choose Always to see the complete ellipses. Atlas compresses radial distance to fit the whole system.

Space

Pause or resume the simulation. F fits all orbits, A switches Atlas, O switches Overview, R resets the view.

1 — 5

Jump between hour, day, week, month and year per second.

Worlds

Use Worlds beside the orbit heading to show or hide markers, labels and orbit lines. The center world always stays visible. Press V to open it.

History

Relative windows are multiples of each body's automatic window — one orbit or about 2.5 synodic loops — from 0.5× to 50×. Fixed windows give every body exactly 1, 10, 50 or 200 years, making travel over the same period directly comparable. The control applies to Solid and Fading; Always ignores it and draws the full orbit.

Esc

Close menus and dialogs. ? reopens this guide.

About the model

Planetary positions come from JPL's Keplerian elements for the mean ecliptic and equinox of J2000, with Kepler's equation solved for every frame. The Moon uses a truncated lunar theory. Distances in Orbits are true; marker sizes are exaggerated for visibility. Atlas compresses radial distance logarithmically, keeping the orbital angles intact. Always draws each world’s complete orbit around its parent — planets around the Sun, the Moon around Earth — translated into the current center’s frame at full brightness, with no fading. This is an exploratory orrery, not a precision ephemeris.
